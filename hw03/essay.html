Where does insecurity come from?

Caesar against a toy EC-ElGamal on secp256k1 · Statistics · MSc Cybersecurity, Sapienza · Homework 3 · October 2026

Two ciphers two thousand years apart, and both broken on these pages. The Caesar cipher shifts every letter by the same secret amount; the toy scheme encrypts with EC-ElGamal on the curve Bitcoin uses, but draws its private key from a small range. Both fall in seconds — for opposite reasons. This essay describes the two schemes, measures what their ciphertexts reveal, breaks each one, and sorts the causes: a key space small enough to search, or a ciphertext that carries the statistics of its plaintext.

Each claim is labelled with its evidence: theorem a proved result · measurement computed on these pages, with a link · historical record with a source · opinion the author’s judgement.

1 · Introduction

The Caesar cipher is the classic first example of encryption: Suetonius reports that Julius Caesar wrote to his friends with every letter replaced by the one three places later in the alphabet historical record [1]. Elliptic-curve cryptography is what protects most key exchanges and every Bitcoin signature today. Putting a toy version of the second next to the first is useful because the two share more than it seems — both add a secret mask in a group — and because each fails for a reason the other does not have.

The homework asks to encrypt and decrypt one message with both schemes, compare them, break Caesar statistically and the toy ECC scheme through its restricted key space, and explain why the second attack does not carry over to properly configured ECC. The pages of this homework do each of those things live in the browser; this essay collects the results and the argument.

2 · The two schemes

Why EC-ElGamal

Part 1 of the previous homework defined key generation on secp256k1: a private key d and a public key Q = dG. A curve and a key pair do not encrypt anything by themselves. To encrypt a message the scheme is completed with EC-ElGamal, the standard public-key encryption built on the same group [2, 3]: it uses exactly those keys, Q to encrypt and d to decrypt, and adds only a fresh random nonce k per message. Real systems use its hybrid form — ECIES or HPKE: Diffie–Hellman for a key, then a symmetric authenticated cipher for the data [4]. The textbook form is kept here because it makes the comparison with Caesar exact.

The clearest way to see why ElGamal looks the way it does is a progression in three steps, each fixing one defect of the previous one (Toy ECC page, first panel):

stepciphertextthe maskwhat breaks it
1. Caesarcᵢ = mᵢ + k in ℤ₂₆fixed, secret, 26 valuesthe same mask on every letter, and only 26 of them

Step 2 shows that a huge group buys nothing if the mask is public. Step 3 makes the mask secret with Diffie–Hellman — S = kQ = k(dG) = d(kG) = dC₁, so the sender (who knows k) and the receiver (who knows d) reach the same point without exchanging a secret — and fresh, with a new k each time theorem. Caesar fails on both counts.

Caesar

Letters are numbers A = 0, …, Z = 25; the key is k ∈ ℤ₂₆, shared by both sides. Encryption is cᵢ = (mᵢ + k) mod 26, decryption mᵢ = (cᵢ − k) mod 26. The implementation shifts only the letters A–Z, keeps their case and leaves spaces, digits and punctuation alone — a design choice that leaks word boundaries. The key space has 26 elements, about 4.7 bits, and k = 0 changes nothing (Caesar page).

Toy EC-ElGamal

The curve is secp256k1, y² = x³ + 7 over a 256-bit prime field, with a generator G of prime order n ≈ 2²⁵⁶ [5]. The roles of the values:

  • d, the private key, known only to the receiver, decrypts. In the toy it is drawn with the browser’s CSPRNG from [1, 2²⁰] by default instead of [1, n − 1].
  • Q = dG, the public key, lets anyone encrypt to the receiver.
  • k, the nonce, known to the sender and then destroyed, makes every encryption different. It is full size and drawn fresh for every message chunk.
  • S = kQ, the shared secret, is the one-time mask.

To encrypt, the message bytes are cut into chunks of 29 bytes and each chunk is turned into a curve point M by Koblitz’s method [6]: the chunk becomes an integer m, and x = 256·m + j is tried for j = 0, 1, … until x³ + 7 is a square mod p. About half of all x work, so the number of tries is Geometric(½) with mean 2; over 3 000 chunks the measured mean was 2.008 measurement. Then C₁ = kG and C₂ = M + S. To decrypt, the receiver checks that both points lie on the curve, computes S′ = dC₁, which equals S, and recovers M = C₂ − S′. The Toy ECC page shows every value of one encryption and its decryption.

3 · Statistical properties

Shifting every letter by k moves the bars of the letter histogram and changes none of their heights: Pcipher(ℓ) = Pplain(ℓ − k). Every statistic that depends only on the multiset of letter counts is therefore identical for plaintext and ciphertext: the index of coincidence, the entropy of the letter distribution, the sorted frequency profile theorem. For the 428-letter Frankenstein paragraph used on these pages, both texts have IC 0.0665 and entropy 4.109 bits per letter, for every one of the 26 keys measurement (Caesar page). Caesar is also deterministic — repeated words stay repeated, double letters stay double — and has no diffusion: one changed plaintext letter changes exactly one ciphertext letter.

The reference English comes from six public-domain novels of Project Gutenberg [7], normalised the way the ciphertexts are (accents removed, upper case, letters only). Four books train the model, 2.54 million letters with E 12.5 %, T 9.0 %, A 8.1 %, O 7.5 %, IC 0.0654 and entropy 4.17 bits per letter; two others (Frankenstein, Dracula) are kept for testing. All six agree on the IC to within ±0.0004 measurement — the letter distribution is a property of the language, not of the book.

EC-ElGamal is the opposite. Because k is fresh, the same message encrypted twice gives two unrelated ciphertexts, which public-key encryption must do: anyone holding Q can encrypt, so a deterministic scheme would let an attacker encrypt guesses and compare. If S = kQ behaves like a uniform point — the decisional Diffie–Hellman assumption — then C₂ = M + S is a one-time pad in the curve group and textbook ElGamal is IND-CPA secure [8] theorem. On these pages the bytes of many ciphertexts of the same short message (the coordinates of C₁ and C₂, without the SEC prefix byte, which would spike the histogram) are flat: about 7.99 bits per byte, and χ² tests against uniform gave p-values between 0.19 and 0.93 in the runs made measurement (Toy ECC, Compare). The Caesar ciphertext keeps the shape of English; the ElGamal ciphertext keeps nothing to count.

4 · Breaking Caesar with statistics

One search, several recognisers

With 26 keys, exhaustive search is free; the whole difficulty is recognising the right decryption without a human. The attack decrypts with every key and scores each candidate with a recogniser (Break Caesar page). Brute force, frequency analysis and n-gram scoring are therefore not three attacks but one search with different recognisers. Six were compared:

  • χ² distance between the decrypted letter counts and those English would give, used as a distance rather than a test — on short texts most expected counts are below 5 and the χ² law does not apply;
  • log-likelihood of the decryption under independent English letters, ln L(k) = Σ Oℓ(k) ln pℓ: the maximum-likelihood estimate of the key, and with a uniform prior also the most probable key;
  • correlation Σ qℓ(k) pℓ of the decrypted and the English frequencies;
  • bigram and quadgram log-likelihoods, which also see the order of letters (“TH”, “TION”), with a floor for n-grams never seen in training;
  • the textbook shortcut “most frequent letter = E”, as a baseline.

The χ² and likelihood methods are closely related. The G-test statistic expands to G = 2[Σ O ln O − N ln N − Σ O ln p]; a shift only permutes the counts O, so the first two terms do not depend on the key and arg min G = arg max ln L exactly; for large N, χ² ≈ G theorem. The page checks the equivalence on every pair of keys. The likelihood methods also give a confidence: Bayes’ rule with a uniform prior turns the 26 likelihoods into a posterior probability for each key.

The experiment

For each length L of 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200 and 500 letters, 500 random excerpts were cut from the test books and encrypted with uniform random keys; every recogniser attacked the same excerpts (a paired design), ties for the best score were broken at random, and each success rate received a Wilson 95 % interval. Three things stand out. The n-gram recognisers need about 10 letters where the letter-count ones need 20–30, and the shortcut is still wrong 5 % of the time at 500 letters. At one letter the unigram methods already beat a blind guess (12 % against 1/26 ≈ 4 %), because they bet on E, which is 12 % of English; the n-gram methods cannot score a text shorter than their n and fall back to chance — the flat start of the quadgram line. And χ² and the log-likelihood nearly coincide, as the expansion above predicts, with the likelihood slightly ahead on short texts, where χ² is a poor approximation.

Measured against theory

Shannon’s unicity distance U = H(K)/D [9] estimates how many letters leave a single plausible key: H(K) = log₂26 ≈ 4.70 bits of key, and every letter gives away D = log₂26 − H bits, the redundancy the attacker’s model can see. Measured on the training books, the entropy of a letter is 4.17 bits on its own, 3.61 given the previous letter, 3.17 given two, and 2.69 given three, so U is 8.9, 4.3, 3.1 and 2.3 letters for models that see single letters, pairs, triples and runs of four measurement (plug-in estimates; the four-letter one is slightly low, since 2.5 million letters cannot fill 457 000 cells). With the entropy rate of English as a whole, roughly 1–1.5 bits per letter by Shannon’s estimates, U would be about 1.5 letters (the author’s notes; to check in the primary source). The recognisers need a few times more than their U, as expected of a lower bound for an ideal attacker measured against a demanding 95 % threshold; but the ordering is exactly the predicted one — the more of the language a model captures, the fewer letters it needs.

Limitations

The attack is ciphertext-only — it needs no known plaintext and no access to the encryption — and its one requirement is enough typical text. Five texts encrypted with k = 11 show where it bends measurement (Where it fails): a list of Polish and Swedish names and codes defeats all six recognisers; a 12-letter message defeats correlation and the shortcut; source code is broken only by quadgrams; a text written without the letter E fools only the shortcut; and Italian, scored with the English model, is still broken by every full recogniser. These are limits of the recogniser, not of the search: a person reading the 26 candidates would break all five at once. The cipher itself offers no protection.

5 · Breaking the toy key by search

An exact test

The attacker knows the curve, G, the public key Q and — by Kerckhoffs’s principle — that d ∈ [1, N]. Against Caesar the recogniser was statistical and could be fooled. Here it is exact: a guess d′ is right if and only if d′G = Q, with no false positives, and no ciphertext is needed at all. The whole difficulty is the number of candidates (Break toy ECC page).

Three searches

  • Brute force walks G, 2G, 3G, … with one point addition per candidate and stops at Q: on average (N + 1)/2 candidates and no memory.
  • Baby-step giant-step [10] writes d = i·m + j with m = ⌈√N⌉, stores the baby steps jG, and walks the giant steps Q − i·mG until one of them is in the table: about 2√N additions, and √N points of memory.
  • Pollard’s kangaroo [11, 12] lets a tame kangaroo start from a known point and a wild one from Q, both jumping by amounts that depend only on the point they stand on; once the wild one lands on the tame one’s trail it follows it, and a shared stored point gives d. About 2√N hops and only a few dozen stored points.

On one 20-bit key (d = 802 491), in Chrome on the author’s laptop measurement: brute force tried 802 491 candidates in about 1 s, at roughly 750 000 additions per second; BSGS needed 1 807 additions and 1 023 stored points (31 ms); the kangaroo 2 010 hops, about 2.0√N, with 26 stored points (49 ms). Each then decrypted the shared message. On a 30-bit key BSGS needed 62 908 additions and 32 767 points (170 ms).

How the work grows

The experiment attacks seeded keys at every size from 2¹⁰ to 2²² (brute force) and 2³² (BSGS, kangaroo), one key per equal slice of the range, and fits the slope of log(additions) against log(N) (seed 1). The slopes were 1.03, 0.50 and 0.48 — theory says 1, ½ and ½ measurement. In time, BSGS grew a little faster than its additions (slope about 0.63): its table grows with √N, and bigger hash tables mean more cache misses and garbage collection — the memory price of the method appearing as time.

Why it does not scale to real ECC

At the measured rates, a 64-bit key range would take brute force about 4·10⁵ years in this browser, and BSGS about 6 hours with 137 GB of table; at 128 bits BSGS would need about 3·10⁶ years and 6·10⁸ TB of memory measurement (extrapolated). A properly generated key has d uniform in [1, n − 1], and the best generic attack, Pollard’s rho, needs √(πn/2) ≈ 2^128.3 additions — about 1.4·10¹⁹ years at a generous 10¹² additions per second, a billion times the age of the universe. Three facts close the remaining doors:

  • no generic algorithm can do much better: in the generic group model any algorithm that only adds and compares points needs on the order of √n operations [13] theorem;
  • for well-chosen curves such as secp256k1 no faster, curve-specific method (such as the index calculus that works in multiplicative groups of finite fields) is known — why 256-bit ECC is considered comparable to 3072-bit RSA, about 128-bit security historical record;
  • and there is nothing statistical to exploit instead: d is uniform and the ciphertexts look uniform (section 3).

The one known exception is a large fault-tolerant quantum computer: Shor’s algorithm solves the discrete logarithm in polynomial time [14], which is why post-quantum key encapsulation (ML-KEM, standardised by NIST in 2024 [15]) is being deployed alongside elliptic curves.

Both schemes add a mask in a group, and that statement is exact: c = m + κ in a finite abelian group theorem. What differs is the group, how the mask is chosen, and how hard it is to compute from public data. Caesar breaks through recognition — searching is free and the work is telling English from noise; the toy ECC breaks through search — recognising is exact and free, and the work is the number of candidates. The Caesar attack exploits the redundancy of the plaintext language; the ECC attack exploits only the smallness of the key space.

The 2 × 2 table

The two causes are independent, and a cipher must avoid both (Compare page):

A large key space alone is not enough — the general substitution cipher has more keys than DES (2⁵⁶) and falls to frequency analysis — and hiding the statistics alone is not enough either — the toy ElGamal hides them perfectly and falls to search opinion, the centrepiece of the comparison.

Key space or design?

Only two of the weaknesses an EC-ElGamal deployment can have are about the size of a key space: a private key or a nonce drawn from a small range. Only those are cured by a larger key. The others survive a 256-bit key unchanged, because they belong to the design or the code: reusing a nonce, drawing it from a predictable generator, encrypting character by character under one nonce (which would turn the scheme back into a substitution cipher, the Caesar problem in a larger group), accepting ciphertexts that can be altered without the key (textbook ElGamal is malleable), not validating received points, and letting the running time depend on the key. Of these, the toy has the restricted private key on purpose, the malleability of the textbook form, and code that is not constant-time; it avoids the rest by construction — fresh full-size nonces from the CSPRNG, multi-byte chunks and validated points (the table on the Compare page). Nonce failures are in fact the classic way elliptic-curve keys have been lost, mostly in ECDSA signatures rather than encryption, with the Sony PS3 signing key (2010) and Android Bitcoin wallets (2013) as the usual examples historical record (the details should be checked in primary sources before being cited).

7 · Final considerations

The question in the title has two answers, and the two schemes illustrate one each: insecurity comes from a key space small enough to search, or from a ciphertext that still carries the statistics of its plaintext. Caesar has both problems; the toy ECC only the first, and only because it was built that way.

A deployable public-key encryption scheme needs more than a large key opinion:

  • the full key space: d uniform in [1, n − 1];
  • a fresh, uniform nonce for every encryption, from a cryptographically secure generator;
  • the hybrid form — ECDH, a key-derivation function and an authenticated cipher (ECIES, HPKE [4]) — which also removes malleability and the need to encode messages as points;
  • validation of every point received;
  • constant-time arithmetic, so that the running time says nothing about the key;
  • and, looking ahead, a post-quantum or hybrid key exchange, since Shor’s algorithm would undo the √n wall that this homework measured.

The comparison also has a statistical lesson. The Caesar attack is an estimation problem — a maximum-likelihood estimate of the key whose error rate depends on the sample size, which is why it was tested with confidence intervals over text length. The ECC attack is not statistical at all; its cost is a deterministic function of the key space, and its only randomness is in which key one happens to face. That is exactly what modern cryptography aims for: nothing left to estimate, and too much to search.
