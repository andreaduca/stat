Caesar vs toy EC-ElGamal

Two ciphers from two eras, one question: where does insecurity come from? Caesar adds a fixed secret shift to every letter; textbook EC-ElGamal on secp256k1 adds a fresh secret point to every message. Both are broken on these pages — Caesar by the statistics of the language it leaks, the toy ElGamal by the small range its private key is drawn from.

Educational toy. The private keys are deliberately small, the code is not constant-time and the textbook scheme is malleable: never use it to protect anything. Keys and nonces come from crypto.getRandomValues; everything runs in your browser and nothing is sent anywhere.

The exercise

Using the toy scheme on the secp256k1 curve with a restricted private key, encrypt and decrypt a simple message and describe the steps and the role of the keys. Implement the Caesar cipher on the same message, and compare the two schemes — their procedures, key spaces and statistical properties. Given a large corpus of a known language, recover the Caesar key from the letter statistics, test the attack on ciphertexts of different lengths and discuss its effectiveness and limits, comparing exhaustive search, letter frequencies and n-grams. Then attack the toy scheme through its restricted key space, contrast that attack with the statistical one, explain why it does not carry over to properly configured ECC, and say which weaknesses come from the key space and which from the design.

The pages, in reading order

  • Toy ECC Why EC-ElGamal (Caesar → naive M + Q → ElGamal), the roles of the keys, one encryption and decryption step by step, “encrypt again”, and the byte histogram of the ciphertext.
  • Caesar The same message under a shift cipher: two sliding alphabets, plaintext and ciphertext histograms, the statistics encryption leaves unchanged (IC, entropy, sorted frequencies), repeats and word lengths, and the English model built from six books.
  • Break Caesar The attack as its two steps — all 26 keys, then a recogniser scoring each decryption — with six recognisers (χ², likelihood, correlation, bigrams, quadgrams, “top letter = E”), the unicity distance measured from the corpus, the success-rate-against-length experiment with confidence bands, and the texts where it fails.
  • Break toy ECC The restricted key recovered from the public key alone: brute force, baby-step giant-step and Pollard’s kangaroo run live with a picture of each search; the message decrypted; the growth of their work measured (N against √N) and extrapolated to real key sizes.
  • Compare The two schemes side by side, the same message as two ciphertext histograms, the 2 × 2 table of key space against statistical leakage, which weaknesses come from the key space and which from the design, and y² = x³ + 7 over 𝔽₉₇ as an illustration.
  • Essay The argument in prose, with the measured numbers and the evidence behind each claim.

The results in four lines

  • Caesar leaves the shape of English intact: index of coincidence and entropy are identical for plaintext and ciphertext, for every key.
  • From the ciphertext alone, quadgram scoring finds the key 95 % of the time from 10 letters; the “most frequent letter = E” shortcut is still wrong 5 % of the time at 500.
  • The toy key falls to search from the public key alone: the work grows like N for brute force and like √N for baby-step giant-step and the kangaroo (measured slopes 1.03, 0.50, 0.48).
  • With a full-size key the same searches need about 10¹⁹ years. A small key space and leaked statistics are two independent ways to fail; a secure scheme must avoid both.
