Three numbers per attacker

Welford's recurrences, the sampling mean and variance, and m hackers × n systems · Statistics · MSc Cybersecurity, Sapienza · Homework 5 · November 2026

A defender who watches attacks arrive one at a time cannot keep every log line forever, and does not need to: the mean and the variance of a stream can be kept in three numbers, updated as each value arrives, exactly and stably. This essay derives that update the way Knuth derives the running mean, shows why the textbook formula fails where it does not, and then asks what the sample mean and variance of each attacker are worth: on average over many attackers, for one attacker observed on few systems, under heavy tails, under an adversary who changes slowly, and when the summaries must be shared without exposing the data behind them.

Each claim is labelled with its evidence: theorem proved in the notes, or an exact computation · measured on these pages, with a link to the run · source from the literature, to be checked before citing · opinion the author's modelling judgement.

1 · Introduction

HW4 modelled penetration as a random walk: n systems, m attackers, an independent step per system. Its average score S_n/n is exactly the sample mean \bar x of this homework, so the law of large numbers proved there is the consistency of \bar x here. This homework adds two things. First, an algorithm: how to keep the mean and the variance of a stream without storing it. Second, the second estimator: the sample variance s^2, what it estimates, how noisy it is, and what that means for inference.

The model replaces HW4's 0/1 or ±1 steps with a score between 0 and 10 per attempt, drawn from a fixed distribution. The default is 10\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(2,5): most attempts achieve little, a few a lot, with \mu = 20/7 \approx 2.857, \sigma^2 \approx 2.551, skewness 0.60 and excess kurtosis \kappa = -0.12. That choice is an assumption, not data: real penetration-test outcomes are not documented well enough to fit one opinion. So the pages show which conclusions do not depend on it (the averages) and which do (the spread of s^2, through \kappa). The home page shows both questions in one picture.

2 · Ex. 1: Welford's recurrences

The derivation. Knuth's idea for the running mean is not to recompute but to correct: kM_k = (k-1)M_{k-1} + x_k, so with the innovation d_k = x_k - M_{k-1},

M_k = M_{k-1} + \frac{d_k}{k}, \qquad x_k - M_k = \frac{k-1}{k}\,d_k .

The sum of squares S_k = \sum_{i \le k}(x_i - M_k)^2 yields to the same idea with one identity, the shift decomposition \sum (x_i - c)^2 = \mathrm{SS} + n(\bar x - c)^2. Split S_k into the old points, now measured from the new centre, and the new point; apply the identity to the old points with c = M_k:

S_k &= S_{k-1} + (k-1)(M_k - M_{k-1})^2 + (x_k - M_k)^2 \\ &= S_{k-1} + d_k\,(x_k - M_k) = S_{k-1} + \frac{k-1}{k}\,d_k^2 ,

and, dividing by k-1, s_k^2 = \frac{k-2}{k-1}s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{d_k^2}{k} theorem. The same identity gives Bessel's correction (with c = \mu), the merge of two summaries and the sensitivity of s^2 for differential privacy: one lemma, four results.

The picture. The increment of S is the product of the old deviation and the new one, the area of a rectangle with sides |d_k| and \frac{k-1}{k}|d_k| inside the square d_k^2. It splits into the new point's own square (x_k - M_k)^2 and a strip (k-1)(M_k - M_{k-1})^2, the extra spread of the old points around the centre that has just moved; the band d_k^2/k left out is what the moving mean absorbs. On the preset 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 the recurrence ends at M = 5, S = 32, s^2 = 32/7 (Welford page, E1, where the number line and the rectangle follow a step slider).

The code. The whole algorithm is three statements, and the first value needs no special case, because from (0, 0, 0) it gives M_1 = x_1 and S_1 = d_1\cdot 0 = 0:

const d = x - this.mean;          // innovation: deviation from the OLD mean
this.mean += d / ++this.n;        // Knuth: move 1/n of the way towards x
this.ss += d * (x - this.mean);   // Welford: old deviation × new deviation

Why bother. The textbook formula (\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2/n)/(n-1) is exact on paper and fails on a machine: when the data share a large offset, its two terms agree in almost every digit and the subtraction keeps only their rounding errors. On the four numbers 10^9 + \{4, 7, 13, 16\}, whose variance is 30, it returns −170.67, a negative variance; two-pass and Welford return 30 measured. On 1 000 values c + U(0,1) with c = 10^8 the relative errors are about 10^2 for the naive formula, 1.8\cdot 10^{-8} for Welford and 1.7\cdot 10^{-14} for two-pass (E3, exact reference in integer arithmetic) measured. The orders follow the known bounds, nuK^2, nuK and nu with the condition number K = \sqrt{1 + n\bar x^2/\mathrm{SS}} source. The offset is not exotic in security: four Unix timestamps around 1.7·10⁹ s already give the naive formula a relative error above 1 000.

Efficiency. Welford needs one pass and three numbers of memory, works on streams that never end, has the variance available at any moment, and merges across machines. Two-pass is more accurate but must read the data twice; the naive formula is one-pass but unstable. Welford is not the fastest per value: in the browser benchmark it takes several times longer than a plain summing loop (about 12 against 2 ms per million values), because of one division per value and a dependency between steps measured. That is a constant factor on an O(n) job, tens of millions of values per second; what it buys is stability, a single pass and constant memory opinion.

3 · Ex. 2: the sampling mean and variance

Fix a distribution, draw R samples of size n, compute \bar x_r and s_r^2 for each, and average. The theory predicts \mathbb{E}\,\bar x = \mu, \mathbb{E}\,s^2 = \sigma^2 and, for the divisor-n variance, \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SS}/n] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 theorem. With R = 10 000 samples of n = 10 (Sampling page):

  • N(0, 1): \overline{\bar x} = -0.0015 (z = −0.46) and \overline{s^2} = 1.0005 (z = 0.10); \overline{\mathrm{SS}/n} = 0.900, on its target 0.9 and 23 standard errors away from \sigma^2;
  • Exp(1): \overline{\bar x} = 0.994 (z = −1.8), \overline{s^2} = 0.986 (z = −1.5);
  • the score: \overline{\bar x} = 2.856, \overline{s^2} = 2.548 (z = −0.24, −0.25) measured.

The comparison has to be made in units of standard error, \sigma/\sqrt{nR} for the mean and \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(s^2)/R} for the variance: "close" is otherwise meaningless. The running averages close in on the truth inside a ±2 SE funnel; the running average of \mathrm{SS}/n converges just as well, to the wrong line.

The sampling distributions say more than their averages. The histogram of \bar x keeps its centre, narrows like \sigma/\sqrt{n} and turns normal (the CLT of HW4). The distribution of s^2/\sigma^2 is centred at 1 for every shape, but its width is \sqrt{2/(n-1) + \kappa/n}: at n = 10, 0.47 for normal data, 0.91 for the exponential, 0.32 for the uniform theorem (measured 0.469, 0.864, 0.322). For skewed data the two estimators are correlated, \operatorname{Cov}(\bar x, s^2) = \mu_3/n: 0.70 for Exp(1) at n = 10, against 0.704 measured measured.

Why this matters for inference. We never know \mu and \sigma^2; we have one sample. Unbiasedness is the guarantee that the estimator has no systematic error over repetitions, which is what these averages make visible. The spread of the sampling distribution is the uncertainty of one estimate, and \mathrm{SE}(\bar x) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} is estimated through s, so the two estimators work together. Known sampling distributions give intervals and tests. And unbiasedness is what licenses pooling: averaging unbiased estimates from many sources converges to the truth, averaging biased ones converges to the wrong value however many are collected theorem. The n-1 is not a convention; it is the correction that makes pooling work. For a single estimate the goal may differ: for normal data the mean squared error of \mathrm{SS}/c is smallest at c = n + 1.

4 · The hacker experiment

m = 2 000 hackers each attack n = 10 systems; Welford per hacker gives \bar x_j and s_j^2 (Hackers page, E5). Across hackers, \overline{\bar x} = 2.852 against \mu = 2.857 (z = −0.42) and \overline{s^2} = 2.540 against \sigma^2 = 2.551 (z = −0.41); the spread of the \bar x_j is 0.265 against \sigma^2/n = 0.255, that of the s_j^2 1.34 against \operatorname{Var}(s^2) = 1.37 measured. With n = 2 the averages are still on target (2.864 and 2.419, within their much larger standard errors): unbiasedness holds at every n theorem.

Three conclusions follow. One hacker tells little and many hackers tell a lot: a single s_j^2 from ten systems has a relative standard deviation of 46 %, the average over 2 000 hackers about 1 % theorem. The two averages answer different questions: \overline{\bar x} is the typical success of the population, \overline{s^2} the typical inconsistency of one hacker across systems. And the comparison with \mu and \sigma^2 is itself a test of the assumption that hackers are alike: if they were, the variance of the \bar x_j would be about \overline{s^2}/n, and the ratio here is 1.04.

When hackers differ. Let 70 % be novices, 10\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(2,5), and 30 % experts, 10\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(5,2), each for all their systems (E6). The law of total variance splits a score's variance into within-group 2.551 and between-group 3.857, total 6.408 theorem. The average sample variance measures only the first: 2.561 against the within 2.551, 143 standard errors from the total. The sample means spread with variance 3.93, against \text{between} + \text{within}/n = 4.11; the run drew 27.6 % experts instead of 30 %, and with that drawn share the theory gives 3.92, a z of 0.09 measured. The ratio \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar x_j)/(\overline{s^2}/n) is 15.3 instead of 1: the excess is the skill gap. This is the idea behind one-way ANOVA; the formal F-test is a next step.

5 · The effect of n

\operatorname{Var}(\bar x_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(s_j^2) = \sigma^4\left(\frac{2}{n-1} + \frac{\kappa}{n}\right) .

Centres do not move with n; spreads fall like 1/n. Repeating the experiment for n from 2 to 1 000 (Effect of n page, E7) gives fitted log-log slopes of −0.995 for the means and −1.02 for the variances, with all ten points of each within 3 standard errors of the exact curves measured. The constant differs: \sigma^2 for the mean whatever the shape, about (2+\kappa)\sigma^4 for the variance, so the curves of the four distributions coincide for the mean and separate by their tails for the variance. Small n is special for s^2: with two systems one hacker's variance has a relative SD of 1.39, nearly useless. The biased estimators converge too: at n = 2 the divisor-n variance reports half of \sigma^2 and s is 21 % below \sigma, both biases vanishing as n grows.

For a defender this is a sample-size question. To estimate one hacker's mean score to ±0.1 with 95 % confidence needs n = (1.96\,\sigma/0.1)^2 \approx 980 observed systems; to estimate their variance to ±10 % needs about 1.96^2(2+\kappa)/0.1^2 \approx 720 theorem. More hackers m do not help in judging one hacker; they sharpen only the population averages. Ranking or profiling attackers from a handful of attempts, especially on how "erratic" they are, is statistically fragile opinion.

6 · Uses: intervals, anomalies, merging, privacy

Intervals and coverage. A 95 % interval is a promise about a procedure, and the promise can be counted (Intervals page, E8, 20 000 samples per cell). The t-interval for \mu keeps it as n grows, for every shape: at n = 300 its coverage is 0.947–0.951 for the four distributions; for Exp(1) it rises from 0.902 at n = 10 to 0.948 measured. The χ² interval for \sigma^2 does not: for Exp(1) it covers 0.769 at n = 10, 0.691 at 100 and 0.679 at 300, approaching its limit 2\Phi\!\left(1.96\sqrt{2/(2+\kappa)}\right) - 1 = 0.673; for the uniform it over-covers, 0.998 theorem measured. The interval is built for the normal spread of s^2; the true spread carries the kurtosis, and more data does not fix it. A security report giving a 95 % interval for the variance of heavy-tailed response times may be badly overconfident at any sample size.

Online anomaly detection, and its poisoning. A defender keeps a running baseline of one attacker's scores and alarms when a score is more than three baseline SDs above it (Anomalies page, E9, 300 attackers). In the novice phase the false-alarm rate is 0.4 % per score for Welford and for a baseline frozen after it, 0.7 % for an exponentially weighted baseline with \alpha = 0.05, 2 % with \alpha = 0.2; with \mu and \sigma known it would be 0.35 %, the excess being the cost of estimating the baseline measured. When the attacker becomes an expert, the frozen baseline flags 43 % of the expert scores (exact with known parameters: 0.4305). If the change is a slow ramp over 500 or 3 000 attempts, Welford and the slower exponentially weighted baselines flag none of them, and the fastest (α = 0.2) fewer than 0.1 %: they have learned the attacker's new behaviour as normal measured. Welford does it not by forgetting but by inflating its variance: the mixed history looks merely erratic. The 3σ rule itself depends on the shape: 0.135 % beyond +3σ for normal data, 0.35 % for the score, 1.8 % for the exponential, while the only shape-free bound, Cantelli's, allows 10 % theorem. Defences are frozen or re-validated references, two baselines with different memories, and limits on how fast a baseline may move opinion. The same running statistics drive per-host traffic baselines and side-channel leakage assessment source.

Merging. Two parties holding (n_A, M_A, S_A) and (n_B, M_B, S_B) can produce the exact summary of the union without exchanging data (Chan): M = M_A + \delta n_B/n, S = S_A + S_B + \delta^2 n_A n_B/n with \delta = M_B - M_A, and Welford's update is the case n_B = 1 theorem. Splitting the 20 000 scores into 4 or 64 shards and merging them left to right, as a tree or in a random order reproduces the centralised summary to 2\cdot 10^{-15} relative (Merge & privacy page, E10) measured. No central server needs the raw logs: this is what local-first and federated statistics rest on. But summaries are not private: yesterday's and today's summary of a group, one attempt apart, give that attempt exactly, x = (n+1)M' - nM.

Differential privacy. Privacy needs noise, calibrated to how far one record can move the statistic. On scores clipped to [0, 10] with n public, one record moves the mean by at most 10/n and the variance by at most 100/n; the second bound comes from Welford, since with the other records summarised as (n-1, M', S'), s^2 = \frac{S'}{n-1} + \frac{(v - M')^2}{n} theorem. The Laplace mechanism releases each with noise of scale sensitivity/ε. For a group of 100 and ε = 1 split evenly, the mean gets noise of SD 0.28 against a sampling SE of 0.16, the variance noise of SD 2.83 against a sampling SD of 0.35: this release turned a true variance of 2.27 into a negative number, clamped to 0 (E11) measured. The privacy noise falls like 1/n, the sampling noise like 1/\sqrt{n}: the privacy noise on the mean drops below the sampling noise from about 320 records, on the variance from about 6 800. Privacy is cheap for large groups and expensive for small ones. The page is a simulation; a deployment needs secure randomness, floating-point-safe noise and accounting over repeated releases source, and choosing ε is a policy decision, not a technical one opinion.

7 · Nature

The same three facts appear well beyond hackers (Nature page), one example per property:

  • Averaging suppresses noise like 1/√N. Photon counts are Poisson, so a pixel's signal-to-noise ratio is \sqrt{N}: on the page's shot-noise demo the measured SNR goes from 1.02 at one photon per pixel to 54.9 at 3 000 (√3 000 = 54.8) measured. Retinas pool photoreceptors and astronomers stack exposures for this reason; and pooling stops helping once the noise is correlated, as for neural populations source.
  • Unbiased estimates, pooled, hit the truth, as in Galton's ox-weighing crowd — but only while the errors are independent: herding sets a floor under the crowd's error, as correlated systems did in HW4 source.
  • A variance is a measurement. In noise analysis of ion channels, the variance–mean relation \sigma^2 = iI - I^2/N of a membrane current reveals the single-channel current i and the number of channels N, which cannot be seen directly: the page's simulation recovers 1.17 pA and 511 channels from 1.2 pA and 500 measured. The limitation is the tails: when the fourth moment is infinite, as for earthquake energies or price changes, the sample variance never settles source.

8 · Final considerations

Three numbers per stream are enough to know its mean and variance at any moment, exactly, stably and mergeably, and the same identity that proves this proves why n-1 is the right divisor, how summaries combine and how much one record can move them. Averages of those estimates across many attackers are reliable; the estimates of one attacker observed on few systems are not, and least of all its variance, whose noise is set by the tails. Every use built on these numbers inherits the same structure: intervals for the mean are robust and intervals for the variance are not, adaptive baselines detect changes and can be taught to ignore them, merged summaries are exact and therefore revealing, and private releases are accurate only for large groups opinion. The model is a toy: the score distribution is assumed, and every result rests on independence across systems and attackers, which real campaigns violate (correlated systems, adaptive attackers). Next steps are robust estimators for heavy-tailed scores, bootstrap intervals for the variance, formal tests of heterogeneity, change-point detectors such as CUSUM, and secure aggregation of summaries.
