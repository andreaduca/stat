From wallet seed to Ethereum address

Statistics · MSc Cybersecurity, Sapienza · Homework 2 · October 2026

An Ethereum wallet keeps one secret, written down as twelve or twenty-four words, and turns it into as many accounts as its owner wants. Between those words and the 0x… address pasted into a payment sit six standards and four different kinds of cryptography. This essay follows the pipeline step by step, measures the statistical properties of each step, looks at how wallets have actually been broken, and asks whether the algebra at its heart is the part a much stronger mathematician would break first.

Each claim is labelled with its evidence: theorem a proved result · measurement computed on these pages, with a link to the configuration · historical record with a source · opinion the author’s judgement.

1 · Introduction: the wallet pipeline

The pipeline has seven steps, and every one of them can be reproduced in a browser (pipeline explorer):

  1. Entropy — 128 or 256 random bits;
  2. → mnemonic: the bits plus a short SHA-256 checksum, cut into 11-bit groups, each a word of a 2048-word list (BIP-39);
  3. → seed: PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512 of the words, 2048 iterations, 512 bits (BIP-39);
  4. → master key and chain code: HMAC-SHA512 keyed with the string “Bitcoin seed” (BIP-32);
  5. → private key k: child derivations along the path m/44’/60’/0’/0/i (BIP-32, BIP-44);
  6. → public key Q = kG on secp256k1;
  7. → address: the last 20 bytes of Keccak-256 of Q, and finally the checksummed address, whose letter case is set by another Keccak-256 (EIP-55).

For the public test mnemonic abandon × 11 about the chain ends at 0x9858EfFD232B4033E47d90003D41EC34EcaEda94, the first account every standard wallet shows for it. Every intermediate value on these pages — seed 5eb00bbd…, master key 1837c1be…, private key 1ab42cc4… — matches the official test vectors measurement. The cryptography is not new: the newest primitive in the chain is from 2012. What the wallet standards of 2012–2016 add is engineering — a way for people to back up, derive and type keys without losing money. As section 7 shows, that engineering layer and the layers above it are where the money has been lost.

2 · The six kinds of operation

Each step takes bits and returns bits, which makes them look alike. They answer different questions, and the homework rightly insists on keeping them apart.

kindquestion it answersin the pipelinereversible?
generationwhere does the unpredictability come from?the 128–256 bits of entropy—
encoding (+ checksum)how do people copy bits and catch slips?entropy → words; bytes → hexyes, by anyone
derivationhow does one secret become many, deterministically?PBKDF2 seed; BIP-32 keys; the BIP-44 pathno
public-key cryptographyhow do others verify without being able to act?Q = kG; ECDSA signaturesno (one-way)
hashinghow do I get a short fingerprint with no structure?Keccak-256 → addressno
checksum in an encodinghow do I catch a typo without changing the format?EIP-55 letter case— (adds redundancy)

The distinction that matters most is between generation and derivation. Generation is the only step that creates unpredictability; every later step is a deterministic function, and a deterministic function cannot add entropy: H(f(X)) ≤ H(X) theorem. A wallet built on 128 random bits has at most 128 bits of security, however many 256- and 512-bit values follow (S1, the entropy pipe). Determinism is the point of derivation: the words are written down once, and every key can be recomputed from them forever. It is also why a bad generator cannot be repaired by anything downstream — the lesson of sections 6 and 7.

Three confusions follow from blurring the categories, and appear in published writing about wallets: the address is not the public key but a hash of it; the mnemonic is not encrypted but encoded, so whoever holds the words holds the funds; EIP-55 does not make addresses secure — it detects typos and has no secret, so an attacker can produce a valid casing for any look-alike.

3 · The standards, one by one

BIP-39 (2013): words and seed

BIP-39 contains two separate ideas. The first is an encoding: ENT bits of entropy (128 to 256) get ENT/32 checksum bits from SHA-256(entropy), and the result is read as numbers in base 2048, one word per 11 bits. For 128 zero bits, SHA-256 starts with 0x37, the checksum is 0011, and the words are eleven times “abandon” (index 0) and “about” (index 3). The last word therefore carries 7 entropy bits and 4 checksum bits, so for eleven fixed words exactly 2⁷ = 128 of the 2048 last words are valid (BIP-39 lab) measurement.

The second idea is a derivation: seed = PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512(password = the words, salt = “mnemonic” + passphrase, 2048 iterations, 64 bytes). PBKDF2 accepts any string — a phrase whose checksum fails, or “correct horse battery staple”, still produces a seed. That decoupling is deliberate, because it lets the optional passphrase be anything, and it means the checksum is enforced by wallet software, not by the mathematics. Each passphrase opens a different wallet; none is “wrong”. The 2048 iterations multiply the cost of every guess by 2048, about 11 bits of work: irrelevant against 128 random bits, decisive for a forgotten word. On these pages a whole missing word is recovered in a few seconds — the checksum discards 15 of every 16 candidates before any expensive work, and every survivor costs one PBKDF2 plus a derivation measurement.

BIP-32 (2012): one seed, a tree of keys

The master key and chain code are the two halves of HMAC-SHA512(“Bitcoin seed”, seed). A child is computed from its parent with I = HMAC-SHA512(cpar, data), ki = IL + kpar mod n, ci = IR. The data is 0x00 ‖ kpar ‖ i for a hardened index (i ≥ 2³¹) and the compressed public key ‖ i for a normal one. That one line decides everything that follows. For a normal child, multiplying by G gives Ki = IL·G + Kpar: public children from a public parent, which is what lets a shop hand out fresh addresses from an xpub without holding any key that can spend. The same linearity is a weakness: the xpub plus one leaked child key gives kpar = ki − IL mod n, and from there every sibling. On the BIP-32 lab both happen: five addresses derived from the xpub alone match the private derivation, and the parent key of m/44’/60’/0’/0 is recovered from child 5 measurement. For a hardened child the attack cannot even be written down, because the HMAC input contains the parent private key. An invalid child (IL ≥ n) has probability about 3.7·10⁻³⁹ and has never been observed theorem.

BIP-44 (2014): a map of the tree

BIP-44 gives the levels a meaning — m / 44’ / 60’ / account’ / change / index, with 60 the coin type of Ether — and hardens the top three, so that a leaked account xpub cannot climb to other accounts, other coins or the master key. It does not settle which level counts “accounts”: MetaMask increments the last index, Ledger Live the account level, and older tools a third variant. The same words therefore agree on the first address and disagree from the second on (three wallets side by side) measurement. An “empty” wallet after a migration is usually a path mismatch, not lost funds.

secp256k1: the only step with a public/private relationship

The curve y² = x³ + 7 over the prime field of p = 2²⁵⁶ − 2³² − 977 has a prime number n ≈ 2²⁵⁶ of points, so every point except the identity generates the whole group. The public key is Q = kG: about 256 doublings and 128 additions forward, while the best known way back — the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm — costs about √n ≈ 2¹²⁸ operations. It is the same group law as on the toy curves of Homework 1, only larger. Its 64 bytes carry about 257 bits of information, because y is fixed by x up to its sign; that is why a compressed key needs only x and one parity bit.

Keccak-256: the address

The address is the last 20 bytes of Keccak-256 of the 64-byte public key. The hash compresses, and it removes the algebra: nothing about Q can be computed from the address. An account that has only received funds exposes nothing but a hash; its first outgoing transaction publishes Q for good, because nodes recover it from the signature. Ethereum’s Keccak-256 is not SHA3-256 (section 4).

EIP-55 (2016): a checksum in the letter case

An address was 40 hexadecimal characters with no checksum, so a typo sent funds to a key nobody holds. EIP-55 hides one check bit in the case of each letter a–f: a letter is uppercase when the corresponding hex digit of Keccak-256(lowercase address text) is at least 8. Digits carry nothing, so the number of check bits L follows Binomial(40, 6/16), mean 15. A random typo passes with probability 2^−L, and on average E[2^−L] = (13/16)⁴⁰ ≈ 0.0247 % theorem — eight times 2^−15, because by Jensen’s inequality the letter-poor addresses dominate the average. An all-lowercase address is accepted as “no checksum”, so the check is optional; and it guards against accidents only (address lab).

4 · Historical context, and SHA-2 versus Keccak/SHA-3

The dates tell the story historical record (timeline). Diffie–Hellman (1976) and RSA (1977) founded public-key cryptography; elliptic curves followed in 1985. SHA-2 was published in 2001. Bitcoin (2008) put secp256k1, ECDSA and SHA-256 in a public ledger. The wallet standards arrived within four years — BIP-32 in 2012, BIP-39 in 2013, BIP-44 in 2014, EIP-55 in January 2016 — built entirely from those existing pieces. Keccak won the SHA-3 competition in October 2012; Ethereum launched in July 2015 with Keccak fixed in its protocol; FIPS 202 standardised SHA-3 one month later, in August 2015, with a different padding byte.

That byte is the whole difference between Keccak-256 and SHA3-256: the same permutation, the same rate and capacity, a domain byte of 0x01 for Keccak and 0x06 for SHA-3. One different input bit to the permutation gives unrelated outputs — c5d24601… against a7ffc6f8… for the empty string — and on these pages both come from one function with the padding byte as a parameter measurement.

The two families differ in construction. SHA-2 is a Merkle–Damgård chain: blocks are folded one by one into a chaining value, and the final chaining value is the digest. Keccak is a sponge: a 1600-bit state of which only the 1088-bit rate ever touches input or output, while the 512-bit capacity stays hidden. Their security targets are the same; the practical difference is length extension. Knowing SHA-256(secret ‖ m) and the length is enough to compute SHA-256(secret ‖ m ‖ padding ‖ suffix) without the secret — demonstrated live on the history page, where the forged tag is accepted measurement. A sponge cannot be resumed, because its output is not its state. This is why BIP-32 and PBKDF2 use HMAC rather than a bare hash. NIST wanted SHA-3 after the 2004–2005 attacks on MD5 and SHA-1, as a structurally different reserve in case SHA-2 followed them; it has not. The wallet uses both families, so one cryptanalytic breakthrough would not take down every step.

5 · Why several primitives instead of one

Because each step needs a property the others cannot — or must not — provide (why many primitives). People need words with a checksum, not 256-bit hex: BIP-39. One backup must cover every key, and a server must derive public keys without private ones: BIP-32, which needs the curve’s homomorphism, not a hash. Anyone must be able to verify while only the owner signs, among millions of anonymous verifiers: a public-key scheme, because a hash has no key pair and a MAC needs a shared secret. The identifier should be short and should hide the key’s algebra: a hash. Typos should be caught without changing the format: a checksum squeezed into letter case. Guessing a weak secret should be expensive: key stretching.

This is separation of concerns, and its benefit is that failures stay local: a bug in EIP-55 casing never leaks a key, a weak passphrase does not break secp256k1, a hash collision does not allow signing. It also places algebraic structure exactly where it is needed — signatures and normal derivation — and removes it everywhere else: hardened derivation hides the linear relation, the address hides Q opinion. Taking any piece away shows what it was for: without BIP-32, one backup per key; without hardening, one leak reaches everything; without the hash, every unused address exposes its public key; without EIP-55, every typo goes through.

Avalanche. After the first derivation, a one-bit change of the entropy is indistinguishable from starting over: about half the bits of the seed, the key and the address change, with the spread of a Binomial law. The words behave differently — a flipped bit changes its own word and, 15 times in 16, the checksum in the last word — and the exact law gives 1.886, which the measurement reproduces. The contrast is k ↦ kG alone: k + 1 changes Q a lot but predictably, to Q + G.

Uniformity, and multiple testing. The addresses pass every test once the number of tests is accounted for. But one raw p-value is below 0.05 (the monobit test, p = 0.037), and with 164 tests per sample that is exactly what chance produces. In the author’s own notes the same thing happened with Python: a monobit deviation of z ≈ −2 replicated once on a fresh sample before vanishing in larger ones, and a direct test of Keccak showed no bias. Even a replication can be a fluke; the defences are a correction for multiple tests, sample sizes fixed in advance, more data, and an independent check of the mechanism. For rare events, the analytic formula beats simulation: 100 000 mistyped addresses give only 24 misses, a relative error near 20 %, while (13/16)⁴⁰ is exact.

Search costs are geometric waiting times. Matching k hex characters of an address takes a geometric number of tries, mean 16k, median about 30 % lower; the simulation agrees. Every character multiplies the cost by 16, which is the economics of address poisoning in section 7: eight matching characters cost about two seconds at 2·10⁹ tries per second, all forty cost 2¹⁶⁰.

What the tests cannot see. This is the most important result of the lab. Keys made from a 16-bit seed — a scaled-down copy of the Profanity, Milk Sad and Trust Wallet bugs — produce addresses that pass the same tests (S5a: smallest corrected p = 0.129), although only 65 536 keys are possible. The same generator then falls to enumeration (S5b): the victim’s key is found after 10 655 guesses, in seconds. And a model of the generator that produced Coldcard seeds from 2021 to 2026 (S5c) passes the byte and bit tests even before its final hash (p = 0.687 raw, 0.808 hashed), while the victim’s 24 words are recovered after 2 794 guesses over the unknown timer value. Statistical tests describe the distribution of outputs; the attacker exploits the size of the input space. Only knowledge of the generation process can certify entropy — which is why randomness standards such as NIST SP 800-90B assess the noise source, not the output.

7 · When it went wrong

In the major incidents of the last fifteen years, no wallet primitive was broken historical record (incidents). The losses sit on six layers around the mathematics.

  • Entropy. Brainwallets hashed human phrases into keys and were swept by bots. Profanity expanded keys from a 32-bit seed (Wintermute lost about $160 M in September 2022); Trust Wallet’s extension and Libbitcoin Explorer (“Milk Sad”) seeded a Mersenne Twister with 32 bits. Each time, every downstream value looked random while the key space was 2³².
  • Signing. ECDSA with a reused nonce gives away the key — Sony’s PlayStation 3 used a constant one in 2010; an Android random-number bug made Bitcoin wallets repeat nonces in 2013; and a few biased nonce bits over many signatures let Breitner and Heninger recover hundreds of real keys with lattices (2019).
  • Key handling. The Slope wallet sent seed phrases to a logging server (2022); the Ronin bridge lost about $600 M when enough validator keys were compromised.
  • Contract logic. The DAO’s reentrancy bug (2016) and the Parity multisig failures (2017): cryptography guarantees who signed, not what the program does.
  • Front-end and supply chain. BadgerDAO (2021) served a script asking for unlimited token approvals; a phished account published a wallet drainer in Ledger’s Connect Kit (2023); in February 2025 malicious JavaScript in Safe{Wallet}’s infrastructure altered Bybit’s cold-wallet transaction during signing — about $1.5 B, the largest theft to date. For a DApp user the screen is the trust anchor, hardware wallets often sign blind, and an approval is delegated authority.
  • The human interface. Address poisoning plants a look-alike that matches the first and last characters of a familiar address; in May 2024 one victim lost about $68 M. EIP-55 does not help: the fake has a perfectly valid checksum.

Coldcard, 2026. The clearest recent case is the subject of the author’s note 09. A library guarded its hardware random-number generator with #ifndef MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG — “is the macro undefined?” — where it meant #if !MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG, “is it zero?”. MicroPython always defines the macro, as 0; the board set it to 0 for its own reasons; the guard never fired, and the linker silently chose MicroPython’s fallback generator, Yasmarang, seeded with a chip-ID word XORed with a timer. A second Yasmarang copy, starting from constants printed in the source, was XORed on top, and the result was hashed with SHA-256d. Five years of seeds were valid, uniform-looking and guessable: with the chip-ID word known, only about 80 000 timer values remained. Sweeps began on 30–31 July 2026; reported totals are around 1 400–2 000 BTC historical record. Users who had mixed in at least 50 dice rolls were safe, because an independent source of entropy composes safely. The fix checked the property that mattered — which function ends up in the binary — rather than a flag that stood in for it. Every component was sound; the failure lived in the wiring between three repositories, one macro and the linker. Test the path, not the component.

The list is a biased sample: undetected thefts, keys compromised but not yet used, and losses never reported are missing, and weak-entropy bugs can stay invisible for years — Randstorm affected wallets from 2011–2015 and was disclosed in 2023.

8 · Structure, assumptions and a mathematician AI

The homework’s last question is whether ECDSA, being built on mathematical structure, is more at risk from a much stronger mathematician — an AI, say — than a hash-based construction. “Structure” needs a precise meaning: relations an attacker can compute with. For k ↦ kG they are global and exposed by the interface — (a + b)G = aG + bG, the period n, a group law. A hash has local structure hidden behind many rounds, and no usable global relation by design. On these pages the difference is literal: the equation f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b) held 1 000 times out of 1 000 for the curve, and never for Keccak on secp256k1 (on the toy curve C5, 3 times in 1 000 — chance, about 1 in 139) (S8) measurement.

What theory says. In the generic group model an algorithm that only uses group operations needs about √n steps (Shoup, 1997): anything faster must exploit how elements are represented, and for secp256k1 nothing is known theorem. Signatures exist as soon as one-way functions do (Rompel, 1990), so for signing — Ethereum’s only use of public-key cryptography — structure is not necessary in principle; key agreement, however, cannot be built from a black-box one-way permutation (Impagliazzo and Rudich, 1989), and with only a random oracle Merkle’s puzzles and their quadratic gap are optimal (Barak and Mahmoody, 2009). Some structure is the price of public-key functionality theorem. Finally, a quantum computer finds the hidden period of a group homomorphism in polynomial time (Shor), so RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic curves fall; against unstructured search it gains only a square root (Grover), so hashes survive with doubled sizes theorem.

What a period looks like. On a toy curve with 19 points and Q = 13P, the pairs (a, b) where aP + bQ hits a fixed point form a line, a + 13b ≡ 1. Its two-dimensional Fourier transform is zero except on the line of frequencies v ≡ 13u: all 18 peaks give k = v·u⁻¹ = 13, and on a curve of 139 points all 138 peaks give the chosen k = 100 (S7) measurement. With a hash in place of the group law the spectrum is noise. Classically this costs more than trying every key; it shows the principle Shor’s algorithm exploits, not a speed-up.

What history says. Structured schemes have tended to fail suddenly when one idea arrived — the Merkle–Hellman knapsack (1982), discrete logarithms in small-characteristic fields (2013–14), the multivariate Rainbow (2022, “a weekend on a laptop”), the isogeny-based SIKE (2022, about an hour on one core). Hashes degraded gradually: MD5 and SHA-1 lost collision resistance after years of warnings, while their preimage resistance — the property hash-based signatures need — has held. The outstanding exception is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm on well-chosen prime-field curves: 40 years without an attack better than generic, apart from special weak classes such as anomalous curves historical record. Integers, by contrast, have a statistical regularity curves lack: a random integer is smooth over small primes with probability about Dickman’s ρ(u), which the lab reproduces (29.6 % of 20-bit integers are 1000-smooth against ρ = 30.3 %, 0.45 % of 40-bit ones against 0.47 %; S11) — the reason RSA needs 3072-bit numbers where a curve needs 256.

Structure in this very wallet. Two attacks of this homework exist only because of linearity: the xpub leak of section 3, and ECDSA nonce reuse. Each signature is the linear equation s·k = z + r·d; two signatures with the same nonce give k = (z₁ − z₂)/(s₁ − s₂) and d = (s₁k − z₁)/r, and on these pages that recovers the public test key exactly — as do the related nonces k and k + 1 (S9) measurement. But hash-based signatures have their own fragility, which is state rather than algebra: a Lamport key used t times lets a forger sign a random message with probability (1 − 2^−t)m. With a 32-bit toy hash the forgery rate measured 14.4 % after four signatures (theory 12.7 %) and 87.5 % after eight (88.2 %), every forgery accepted by the public key (S10); for a real 256-bit key it is 2^−106 after two signatures and 37 % after eight. Stateless SLH-DSA avoids the problem with signatures of 8 to 50 KB, against 64 bytes for ECDSA, on a chain where every byte costs fees. Minimal assumptions are not the same as misuse resistance.

So, the mathematician AI. Where could new mathematics bite? First, and by a wide margin, in implementations and systems — entropy, nonces, front-ends, contract logic — where money is lost today and where a model that reads code at scale would find bugs long before it proved a theorem. The Coldcard bug fits the pattern: five years old, no mathematics involved; Coinkite speculated that AI-assisted review sped up its discovery and Galaxy Research reported attackers using AI models, but neither is forensic proof historical record. Second, in structured assumptions, where history shows that breaks happen and where a sufficiently strong mathematician would look for an elliptic-curve analogue of smoothness — Semaev’s summation polynomials are the research attempt, so far without a practical attack. Third, and least plausibly, in hash functions, whose preimage-type properties have been the most robust, and which can be swapped without redesigning the scheme opinion.

My answer, with its evidence levels: for signatures, hash-based schemes rest on fewer and weaker assumptions theorem; ECDSA’s structure is a larger mathematical attack surface — proven fatal against a quantum computer, historically plausible against a new classical idea, and yet unexploited classically for forty years historical record. The price of hashes is size and, for stateful schemes, fragile key management; the price of structure is concentrated mathematical risk. A robust system therefore exposes as little structure as possible — hash-hidden public keys, hardened derivation, deterministic nonces — and keeps a path open to conservative primitives; Ethereum researchers are working on hash-based signatures for the consensus layer for exactly this reason opinion.

9 · Final considerations

Three conclusions run through the whole pipeline.

First, the secret is made once. Generation is the only source of unpredictability; every later step — however many bits it outputs, however random they look — is a function of it. The pipeline’s strength is fixed at the first step and can only be lost afterwards, never added. The Coldcard seeds, the Milk Sad keys and the 16-bit keys of the lab all looked perfect.

Second, statistics describe outputs, not origins. Every test on these pages has the same honest limit: it can confirm that a step behaves like a random function, measure a checksum’s miss rate exactly, or show a period where there should be none; it cannot certify entropy. Good practice is to decide the tests in advance, correct for how many were run, replicate, prefer the formula for rare events, and say which claims are measurements and which are opinions.

Third, the mathematics is the strongest part — and the most concentrated risk. No incident here broke secp256k1, Keccak, SHA-2 or PBKDF2; money was lost in the layers around them. Yet the one place where a much stronger mathematician could cause a sudden, total failure is the algebraic structure that makes signatures and watch-only wallets possible. The wallet pipeline already answers with its design: use structure only where it is needed, hide it everywhere else, and keep the layers separate so that a failure stays local opinion.
