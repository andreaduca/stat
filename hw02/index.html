From wallet seed to Ethereum address

A crypto wallet keeps one secret — a few hundred random bits written down as 12 or 24 words — and turns it into as many accounts as you like. Between the words and the 0x… address you paste sit six standards and four different kinds of cryptography. These pages run every step in your browser, show every intermediate value, and measure the statistical properties of each one.

Every key on these pages is a public test key unless you ask for a random one, which is for demonstration only. Nothing is sent anywhere and no key is ever stored. Never type a real seed phrase into this site, or into any website.

The exercise

Check the pipeline from a BIP-39 mnemonic to an Ethereum address and explain the role of each standard: BIP-39, BIP-32, BIP-44, secp256k1, Keccak-256 and EIP-55. Tell apart key generation and key derivation, public-key cryptography, hashing, checksums and encoding. Place the standards in history, explain why several primitives are combined instead of one, discuss the statistical properties of each step, look at real attacks, and ask whether structured cryptography such as ECDSA is more at risk than hash-based constructions against a much stronger mathematician — with statistics and small simulations as evidence.

How to use these pages

  • Pipeline explorer The seven steps below, one card each, with the formula, the category of the operation and every value in hex. Reveal mode marks what a one-bit change does downstream.
  • BIP-39 lab A live checksum checker, the 128 valid last words, PBKDF2 accepting any phrase, passphrases, and a real forgotten-word recovery.
  • BIP-32/44 lab The key tree, a watch-only wallet working from an xpub alone, the xpub + child-key leak, and three wallets’ paths side by side.
  • Address & EIP-55 lab Keccak-256 against SHA3-256, a typo playground, and how many check bits an address really has.
  • History & hashes The timeline of the standards, Merkle–Damgård against the sponge, and a live length-extension attack.
  • Why many primitives The category matrix, what would break without each primitive, and where structure is wanted or removed.
  • Statistics lab Eight seeded experiments: the entropy pipe, avalanche, address uniformity, checksum miss rates, weak entropy (including the Coldcard model) and vanity waiting times — each against its theory.

The pipeline

Each box is a value, each arrow the operation that produces the next one. The badge is the kind of operation that created the value; click a box to open that step.

  3. Mnemonic12–24 words⇄encoding + checksum
  4. PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512, 2048 rounds, salt "mnemonic" + passphrase

Six kinds of operation

The steps look alike — bits in, bits out — but they answer different questions, and confusing them is the most common mistake in writing about wallets.

kindquestion it answersreversible?
⚄generationWhere does the unpredictability come from? The only step that creates any.—
⇄encoding + checksumHow do I write bits so a person can copy them, and catch slips?yes, by anyone
↘derivationHow do I turn one secret into many, deterministically?no
⊕public-keyHow do others verify without being able to act?no (one-way)
#hashingHow do I get a short fingerprint that hides the structure?no
✓checksum in encodingHow do I catch a typo without changing the format?— (adds redundancy)

Generation is the only source of entropy: every later step is a deterministic function and cannot add any, H(f(X)) ≤ H(X). A wallet built on 128 random bits has at most 128 bits of security, however many 256- and 512-bit values follow.

What to try first

  1. Open the pipeline explorer on the test vector and compare the address with 0x9858EfFD232B4033E47d90003D41EC34EcaEda94, the first account any standard wallet shows for abandon … about.
  2. Switch on reveal mode, flip entropy bit 0, and count how many words, seed bits and address bits change.
  3. Type a passphrase: every value from the seed down changes, and nothing tells you it is “wrong”.
  4. Set index 1 with the MetaMask path, then with Ledger Live: the same words, different second accounts.
