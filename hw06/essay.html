Two ways to measure area, one bell curve

Riemann and Lebesgue integration, and the normal CDF · Statistics · MSc Cybersecurity, Sapienza · Homework 6 · November 2026

Abstract

Riemann measures an area by cutting the domain into cells; Lebesgue by cutting the range into levels and measuring the set where the function exceeds each level. On a collection of eleven functions the two numerical schemes reach the same value whenever the function is Riemann integrable, but at different speeds, and the real cost of the Lebesgue scheme is computing those measures: an error of 10⁻⁶ on \sin x costs 51 evaluations with Simpson's rule and 94 372 with root-found preimages. They disagree only where Riemann's integral does not exist or is improper, and a computer cannot even represent the difference between a rational and an irrational number. The second exercise is an integral with no formula, the normal CDF \Phi. Liouville's theorem proves it is not elementary; a series near 0 and Laplace's continued fraction in the tails compute it to 7.6\cdot10^{-15} relative error on [-37.5, 8.25], checked against 80-digit values and the NIST table, while any method that forms the lower tail as 1 - \Phi(-x) loses every digit beyond x \approx -8.3. That tail is where p-values, lattice-crypto samplers and differential-privacy calibrations live.

Each claim is labelled with its evidence: theorem proved in the notes, or an exact computation · measured on these pages, with a link to the run · source from the literature, to be checked before citing · opinion the author's judgement.

1 · Introduction

Inference rests on two tools, and this homework builds both. The first is the integral itself: a probability is a measure, and an expected value is a Lebesgue integral, so the question of what an integral is sits under every statistical statement. The second is one particular integral, the normal CDF

\Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} \frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt,

which every z-test, p-value and confidence interval evaluates. The first exercise compares the two classical definitions of the integral as algorithms; the second asks how to compute \Phi when, as we will see, no formula for it can exist.

Two methodological rules hold throughout. Every numerical result is shown with its error against a known reference, and every comparison between methods shows accuracy and cost together. Everything runs in the browser in IEEE 754 double precision; the references were computed separately, at 80 digits, in Python's decimal.

2 · Two ways to measure area

Riemann cuts the domain [a, b] into N cells, takes one height per cell and adds height × width. Lebesgue cuts the range: for each level t he asks how much of the domain lies above it, the measure \lambda\{f > t\} of a preimage. The bridge between the two is the layer-cake formula theorem:

\int_\Omega f = m\,\lambda(\Omega) + \int_m^M \lambda\{f > t\}\,dt,

where m and M are the smallest and largest values of f. It turns Lebesgue's integral into an ordinary one-dimensional integral over heights. Any rule in t then gives a Lebesgue-style method; this homework uses the midpoint rule in t (the "layer cake") and Lebesgue's own lower simple-function sum. The two pictures side by side are on the Riemann vs Lebesgue page (E1): rectangles over the domain, slabs over the range, each slab as wide as its preimage.

Lebesgue described the difference with money source: paying a debt by taking coins out of the pocket in the order they come is Riemann; first sorting them by value and paying each heap at once is his integral. The analogy contains a warning that the numerics confirm. If the measures \lambda\{f > t\} are obtained by counting grid points, the layer cake is the midpoint sum regrouped, the same coins in another order: the two agree to 2\cdot10^{-15} relative on \sin x measured (E4). A Lebesgue method is genuinely different only when the measures come from somewhere else: a formula (an analytic inverse), root finding on each monotone piece of f, or random points.

3 · The numerical comparison

The collection (note 01) was chosen so that each member tests one thing: \sin x and x^2 are smooth but flat somewhere; two step functions jump on and off the grid; |x - \frac{1}{2}| has a kink; x^{-1/2} is unbounded; \sin x / x has no elementary antiderivative, so its preimages need root finding; \sin(1/x) oscillates infinitely often near 0.

Same limit. For every Riemann-integrable member the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals are equal theorem, and the numerics agree: at 10^5 cells and levels the two schemes are 5.4\cdot10^{-9} apart on \sin x, exactly equal on the steps, 4.5\cdot10^{-5} apart on \sin(1/x) measured (E2, “Same limit?”).

Different speed. The fitted log-log slopes over N, K \in [10, 1000] reproduce the textbook orders for Riemann: −2.00 for the midpoint rule, −4.00 for Simpson on \sin x. The layer cake on the same function converges with slope −1.47, not −2 measured. The reason is a square root: near a maximum M with f'' \ne 0, \lambda\{f > t\} \approx c\sqrt{M - t}, and the midpoint rule in t loses half an order on the last level theorem. The duality is the main lesson of Ex. 1 opinion: cutting the domain suffers where f is steep, jumps or blows up; cutting the range suffers where f is flat, because a flat stretch puts a lot of domain at nearly one level. The cases confirm it. A jump on a cell boundary costs the midpoint rule nothing, moved to 1/\sqrt{2} it costs every Riemann rule O(1/N), and the layer cake does not care where the jump is. On x^{-1/2} the midpoint rule crawls at N^{-1/2} (1.9\cdot10^{-3} at N = 10^5), while the layer cake on a geometric t-grid with the exact tail 1/T is exact from K = 10 measured (E2 for f5).

Cost. Accuracy alone does not rank methods. Counting every evaluation of f, including those inside the root finder, an error of 10^{-6} on \sin x costs 51 evaluations with Simpson, 1 000 with the midpoint rule and 94 372 with the root-finding layer cake; plain Monte Carlo does not reach it within 10^5 samples measured (E3). For smooth one-dimensional functions Riemann-type rules win, by orders of magnitude. Lebesgue's strengths lie elsewhere opinion: in theory (convergence theorems, a unified treatment of sums and integrals), in functions Riemann cannot handle, and in practice when the distribution of values is known or cheap, as for x^{-1/2}, for steps, and in high dimension (section 7).

4 · Pathological functions

Lebesgue's criterion theorem says that a bounded function is Riemann integrable exactly when its discontinuities form a set of measure zero, and then the two integrals agree. So the approaches can only give different answers when the discontinuities are too many, or when the domain is unbounded and Riemann's integral is only an improper limit (Pathological page).

Dirichlet, 1 on the rationals and 0 elsewhere, is the first case. With rational tags every Riemann sum is 1, with irrational tags every sum is 0, so there is no Riemann integral; the Lebesgue integral is 0 because the rationals are countable, hence null. The interesting part is what a computer does. A double is an integer times a power of two, always rational: 0.1 is stored as 3602879701896397/2^{55} theorem. Evaluated on floating-point tags, the Dirichlet function is 1 everywhere and every sum is 1. Irrational tags can only be represented symbolically, as (i - 1 + \sqrt{2})/N with the \sqrt{2} kept as a name (P1). No measurement of finite precision can tell a rational from an irrational; floating point, like physics, sees functions only up to sets of measure zero, which is exactly the Lebesgue point of view opinion.

Thomae's function (1/q at p/q, 0 at irrationals) is discontinuous at every rational, yet integrable: in exact integer arithmetic the left-tag sum is \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{gcd}(i, N), 0.00850 at N = 1000 and 0.00198 at the prime 1009, and the upper Darboux sums fall to 0.011 at N = 10^4 measured (P2). Number theory appears inside a Riemann sum.

A fat Cantor set is the case that fools numerics. Removing the middle 4^{-n} of every piece at step n leaves a closed set S with no interval inside and measure \frac{1}{2}. Its indicator is not Riemann integrable, yet left, midpoint and random-tag sums at N = 10^5 give 0.5002, 0.4995 and 0.5003: the Lebesgue value measured (P3). Only the Darboux sums, the adversary that picks every tag in a removed gap, show the failure: lower sums stay at 0 while upper sums fall to \frac{1}{2}. Riemann integrability is a worst case over all tags; numerics only ever try typical ones.

On [0, \infty) the roles swap. \int_0^T \sin x/x\,dx settles at \pi/2 (1.570846 at T = 10^4), while \int_0^T |\sin x|/x\,dx grows like \frac{2}{\pi}\ln T measured: an improper Riemann integral exists, a Lebesgue integral does not, because Lebesgue requires \int |f| < \infty. Finally the Cantor function integrates to \frac{1}{2} by both methods, although it climbs from 0 to 1 with derivative 0 almost everywhere: the fundamental theorem of calculus fails for it.

So "the two approaches behave differently" means three things (note 02): different answers only when Riemann's integral does not exist or is improper; the same answers at different difficulty for every Riemann-integrable function; and a blurred line in floating point, which samples a null set.

5 · Computing Φ

The target was full double-precision relative accuracy for every x where \Phi(x) is a normal double, tails included. Relative, because a tail probability is judged against its own size: \Phi(-10) = 7.6\cdot10^{-24}, and an answer with an absolute error of 10^{-16} is no answer. Formed as 1 - \Phi(10) in doubles, the lower tail is 7 % wrong at x = -8 and exactly 0 from x = -9 measured (theory card).

Ten methods were implemented and compared on 2 001 points of [-37.5, 8.25] (E7). Quadrature from 0 (trapezoid, Simpson), the alternating Taylor series of \operatorname{erf}, the positive series and the classical Abramowitz–Stegun formula all form the lower tail by subtraction, and their relative error reaches 1 before x = -9. The Taylor series fails twice: its terms grow to 10^{11.7} at x = 8 before they shrink, and the rounding of those terms swamps a sum of order 1 measured. The methods that compute the tail directly survive: Simpson on the tail (to about 10^{-3} with a fixed N), the Zelen–Severo formula (to its 16 % deep in the tail), and Laplace's continued fraction Q(a) = \varphi(a)/(a + 1/(a + 2/(a + \cdots))), which is excellent for large a (7 iterations at a = 30) and slow near 0 (396 at a = 1).

The production method M7 uses each where it is good theorem: below the switch-over s = 2, the positive series \Phi(a) - \frac{1}{2} = \varphi(a)\sum a^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!!, whose terms are all positive; above it, the continued fraction for the tail itself; and e^{-x^2/2} computed with x^2 split exactly into two pieces. Its worst relative error over the grid is 7.6\cdot10^{-15}, and 2.9\cdot10^{-15} at s = 1.5, at the price of up to 187 instead of 103 iterations measured. A fixed Chebyshev expansion (M8), with 21 + 16 coefficients fitted once at 80 digits, reaches 5.0\cdot10^{-15} with the same work at every x, and is the fastest method with full accuracy, about 90 ns per call against 240 ns for M7 in this browser (E8). An iterative method is easy to make accurate; a fixed polynomial is faster and takes constant time, which matters again in section 7.

Trusted references. The 80-digit values come from two independent methods, the positive series and the continued fraction evaluated backward, which agree to 10^{-68} or better theorem; M7 matches them to 1.5\cdot10^{-15} at every test point. The web reference the exercise asks for, the NIST/SEMATECH table source, gives \Phi(z) - \frac{1}{2} to five decimals: all nine rows checked agree with M7 (E9).

Avoidable and unavoidable error. The condition number \kappa_\Phi(x) = |x\varphi(x)/\Phi(x)| lies between x^2 and 1 + x^2 for x < 0 theorem: near x = -30 a relative change u of the input becomes 900u in the output. That part is unavoidable for a rounded input; the rest is the method's. The textbook route \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(-x/\sqrt{2}) rounds x/\sqrt{2} and loses up to 1.1\cdot10^{-13}, a naive e^{-x^2/2} loses 5.7\cdot10^{-14}, both growing like x^2u, while M7 stays at its 7.6\cdot10^{-15} measured (E10).

6 · Liouville and special functions

Why compute at all, rather than find a formula? Because there is none, and that is a theorem. An elementary function is built from rational functions by arithmetic, composition, \exp, \log and algebraic roots. By Liouville's criterion (1835, in the modern form of Rosenlicht 1972 source), \int f e^{g} with rational f, g is elementary exactly when R' + g'R = f has a rational solution R. For the Gaussian the equation is R' - 2xR = 1, and the proof that it has no rational solution takes two lines theorem. A pole of order k in R becomes a pole of order k + 1 in R' and nothing on the left can cancel it, while 1 has no poles. A polynomial R of degree d makes 2xR of degree d + 1, again with nothing to cancel it, while 1 has degree 0. So \int e^{-x^2}, \Phi and \operatorname{erf} are not elementary. The checker on the Liouville page lets the reader try: every candidate leaves a term behind.

Naming the integral \operatorname{erf} is closure by extension, as \sqrt{-1} was adjoined to the reals: in the enlarged class, \Phi(x) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + \operatorname{erf}(x/\sqrt{2})) is an exact closed form, and the name carries knowledge (series, continued fractions, bounds, the equation y'' + 2xy' = 0). It does not carry digits. Neither does e^x: in this browser Math.exp(1) is a rounded approximation produced by range reduction and a polynomial, like every \Phi value on these pages. The distinction between elementary and special functions is algebraic, not computational opinion; the computational questions are cost, uniform relative accuracy, constant time and safety from underflow. That closed-form-ness is a property of our vocabulary rather than of the function is the same lesson as "every computer number is rational, yet we reason with reals" opinion.

7 · Connections: inference, lattices, privacy, Monte Carlo

Inference. A p-value is a tail area: Q(z) one-sided, 2Q(|z|) two-sided. Under H_0 it is uniform, by the probability integral transform theorem, and 10^5 simulated tests give a flat histogram (χ² test of uniformity, p = 0.19); under an alternative of 2.5 standard errors 70.5 % fall below 0.05, the power measured (E12b). Particle physics announces discoveries at 5σ, a one-sided p = 2.87\cdot10^{-7}, and a Bonferroni correction over a million tests needs z = 5.45 (E12c): decisions live in the far tail, where at 9σ the methods that subtract from 1 report p = 0 and beyond 38σ only a log-space value survives (E12a).

Lattice cryptography. Lattice schemes add discrete Gaussian noise, drawn by looking a random integer up in a cumulative table, a discrete \Phi source. The table's precision is a statistical distance: for \sigma = 2, 5.7\cdot10^{-5} at 16 bits per entry, 1.1\cdot10^{-9} at 32, 4\cdot10^{-19} at 64 with an exact table, each bit halving it; but a 64-bit table built from doubles only reaches 3\cdot10^{-16}, because a double holds 53 bits measured (E13a). The accuracy lesson of Ex. 2 becomes a security lesson. So does its cost lesson: a sampler that stops at the first table entry above the random number makes T + x + 1 comparisons for output x, so its running time reveals the secret sample, while a constant-time sampler always makes 2T + 1 (E13b). A cache-timing attack of exactly this kind broke the BLISS signature scheme source.

Distance and privacy. The statistical distance \frac{1}{2}\int|p - q| is an integral in the sense of Ex. 1 and, for two Gaussians, a value of \Phi in the sense of Ex. 2: 2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1. Simpson integration and the closed form agree to 10^{-14}, and a simulated threshold test reaches the bound measured (E14a). The analytic Gaussian mechanism of differential privacy source gives \delta as a difference of two lower-tail \Phi values. Calibrated exactly, (\varepsilon, \delta) = (0.5, 10^{-5}) needs noise \sigma = 7.03, against 9.69 from the classical formula, 38 % more noise than necessary; computed with 1 - \Phi(-x), \delta is misreported by 6\cdot10^{-3} relative at \delta = 10^{-14} measured (E14b). An inaccurate \Phi misstates a privacy guarantee.

Monte Carlo as Lebesgue. Monte Carlo estimates an integral from the values of f at random points, i.e. from a sample of the distribution of f: the layer cake of that sample is the sample mean. In one dimension the grids win easily (Simpson exact to rounding at 10^6 points, Monte Carlo off by about 10^{-3}), but on the fat Cantor set, where no grid rule is reliable, Monte Carlo gives 0.4997 \pm 0.0005 with an error bar that means what it says (E15a). In ten dimensions a grid with ten points per axis needs 10^{10} evaluations, while 10^5 random points give its volume as 2.80 \pm 0.17 (exact 2.55), an error the standard error honestly announces (E15b). And for a rare event like Q(5), plain sampling needs about 3\cdot10^8 draws for 10 % accuracy, importance sampling from N(5, 1) about 570, its relative variance per sample being e^{25}Q(10)/Q(5)^2 - 1 = 5.68 theorem (E15c), itself a far-tail \Phi computation.

8 · Nature

Three examples from note 05, each labelled by how far it can be trusted. Lakes source: limnologists compute a lake's volume from the areas of its depth contours, V = \int A(z)\,dz, which is the layer-cake formula with f = depth, the Lebesgue integral in use before Lebesgue. The demo measures a synthetic lake both ways: 4.5832 million m³ by columns, the same to 5\cdot10^{-6} by 500 contour slabs measured. Kelvin's erf source: the temperature below a surface suddenly held at a fixed temperature is an erf profile, and in 1862 Kelvin fitted it to the geothermal gradient to give the Earth 20 to 400 million years. The mathematics was right and the model wrong (no radioactivity, no convection): a correct formula with a wrong model gives a wrong answer. 5σ and genome-wide significance source: the Higgs boson at p \approx 2.87\cdot10^{-7} and genetic association at p < 5\cdot10^{-8} are far-tail values of \Phi, where section 5 showed which methods can be trusted. Behind all of them stands the Galton board: many small independent pushes add up to a normal law, and twelve rows of pegs already bring the binomial within 0.005 of the normal curve.

9 · Final considerations

The two exercises turned out to be one argument. Riemann and Lebesgue compute the same areas in different orders, and the order decides where each one struggles: Riemann at steep and broken parts of the domain, Lebesgue at flat parts of the range and in the cost of its preimages. The normal CDF is the case where the area has no formula at all, by a theorem, so the order of computation is everything: the same integral is exact to the last bit or wrong in every digit depending on whether the small tail is computed directly or subtracted from 1. opinion The practical rule that comes out of both is to compute small quantities directly, measure the error against an independent reference, and report accuracy together with cost.

The limits should be stated. The functions are one-dimensional textbook cases, chosen to isolate effects; the timings belong to one browser on one machine; the lattice sampler and the privacy mechanism are simulations of the arithmetic, not deployable components; the lake is synthetic; and the historical and real-world claims are marked as sources still to be checked. The numerical claims are reproducible from the pages, and the references from hw06/tools/reference.py.
